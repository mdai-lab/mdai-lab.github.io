DAI LAB / RESEARCH

Research

The Dai lab develops multiplexed genomics and microscopy technologies to understand cell signaling and tissue organization in the context of development and cancer. We combine a variety of biophysical, biochemical and computational principles, and push limits in all directions to improve sensitivity, accuracy, multiplexity and scalability. Current research directions include method development for ultra-sensitive and scalable single-cell sequencing, single-molecule protein imaging and sequencing, and charting the spatial cancer-immune atlas.

Single Cells

Ultra-sensitive and scalable single-cell sequencing

Current sequencing methods are inefficient and bottlenecked by repeated sampling of highly abundant molecules, which dominate sequencing reads, limit assay throughput and sensitivity for rare targets. We use a unique, biochemical sequence compression approach to enable ultra-sensitive and massively scalable single-cell and spatial profiling.

Current single-cell sequencing methods are severely constrained by sequencing throughput and cost, reflected at both molecular and cellular levels, resulting in shallow gene coverage as well as limited sample throughput. Fundamentally, this is caused by the high dynamic range of mRNA expression in human cells (5-6 logs). We develop a unique approach, "Compression Sequencing", that tackles this key constraint and enables highly improved (>100x) sequencing power.

Our method works by performing an accurate and unbiased logarithmic transform on molecular abundances over a wide (5 logs) dynamic range, thus suppressing high-abundance targets and enriching rare ones, while maintaining quantitative accuracy. Applied to scRNA-seq libraries, our method allows ultra-sensitive detection of low-abundance transcripts (2-5x more UMIs), ultra-low sequencing cost (200x reduction), preserves accurate cell types and differential expression analysis over a 500-2,000 gene panel. In AML clinical samples, Compression Sequencing reproduces clinical diagnosis and additionally allows transcriptomic profiling at affordable cost (est. $10 per sample). Our approach thus enables ultra-sensitive and scalable single-cell analysis for large-scale functional genomics studies, drug discovery screens, AI cell model training, as well as affordable single-cell disease diagnostics.

Protein Seq.

Optical, single-molecule protein sequencing

Sensitive detection and accurate characterization of unknown protein species or complex is key to understanding complex gene regulation, cell signaling and disease progression. In contrast to rapid advancement in DNA sequencing technologies, a general-purpose protein sequencing method that is both sensitive and accurate is still lacking. We combine amino acid labeling and DNA-enabled super-resolution imaging to read out protein sequences and post-translational modifications. The goal is protein sequencing and proteoform is to read information from intact proteins, one molecule at a time.

We use a unique optical sequencing approach that combines super-resolution microscopy (DNA-PAINT) and amino acid signature labelling, and allows ultimate sensitivity and full-protein analysis. Integrating with biophysical protein stretching further allows accurate protein sequencing and proteoform profiling that cannot be achieved by mass spectrometry, nanopore sensing or peptide-based assays.

We have developed a method for high-efficiency (95%) and high-specificity conjugation of DNA oligo on lysine residues in large, intact proteins, that is robust to complex sequence or protein higher-order structures, and compatible with precise single-molecule imaging readout. Using quantitative DNA-PAINT (qPAINT), our method showed accurate and precise fingerprinting (11% CV on single molecules, 0.8 lysine count error) across a wide range of molecular weight (14 - 300 kDa) and >100x range in lysine content. We are working on further extending amino acid coverage, and combining with microfluidic based protein stretching to enable accurate analysis of protein isoforms and PTM patterns, towards high-throughput, single-molecule protein profiling and single-cell proteomics.

Charting Cancer

Charting the molecular atlas of cancer-immune interactions

A central challenge in cancer biology is understanding how the spatial organization of tumors, and cell-cell communication within them, determine whether the immune system prevails. Why do potent immune cells fail to infiltrate or eliminate certain tumors? How do cancer cells learn to silence the immune cells sent to destroy them? Why do some disseminated tumor cells evade immune surveillance to seed metastases, while others are cleared? Answering these questions requires a sensitive, high-resolution map of the tumor microenvironment, one that captures not only which cells are present and where they are, but also what genes and proteins they express, and their full spatial context.

We aim to build a sensitive, high-resolution, molecular map of the tumor microenvironment, one that captures not only which cells are present and where they are, but also what genes and proteins they express, their full spatial context, and how proteins mediating their interactions are organized.

We apply our next-generation single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and super-resolution protein imaging technologies to build this map, linking genome-wide measurements of cell state to 3D mapping of cellular organization, to nanoscale views of the molecular machinery at cell–cell interfaces. Our goal is to uncover the structural rules that govern cancer–immune interactions: how T cells infiltrate tumors and engage their targets, how macrophages are polarized by the local niche, and how physical and molecular barriers enable immune evasion during cancer progression and metastasis.
